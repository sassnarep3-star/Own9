Executive summary

  1. The committed NFL claims hold up. 369 checks were run against the official documents this session read on 2026-11-14. 369 matched, 0 mismatched and 0 could not be checked. Every starting lineup, every inactive list, every final score and every injury-report row committed in the repository matched the league's own document.
  2. The repository has an odds archive it has been reporting as absent. The committed results file carries a closing spread and a closing total for 7424 games spanning 1999 to 2026, and both moneylines for 5443 of them. Part of it was checked against the league: the 2026 season recomputed from the file gives Pittsburgh 6-2-0 with 180 points for and 144 against, and Tennessee 1-7-0 with 144 for and 199 against. The official standings say exactly the same. An earlier flag on the record says no odds archive exists. That flag is wrong, and a wrong flag is worse than none because it stops anyone looking.
  3. But it has no opening price, so half of the price requirement cannot be met. There is no opening column and no timestamp column in any committed file. A store that records only a closing number cannot show that a strategy beat a number that was actually available. This is a source problem, not a code problem, and it needs an owner decision.
  4. The results feed was two rounds stale, and this session closed the gap and proved it. 137 of the 272 scheduled 2026 games had been played by 2026-11-14. 16 of them carried no result: the entire Week 9 slate plus the Thursday-night game the league published two days earlier. Before the gap was filled only two of 32 clubs could be reconciled against the official standings, and only because Pittsburgh and Tennessee were on their bye in Week 9, which the league's own schedule page confirms. This session read all 16 outcomes from the league's pages and then applied them back: with the sixteen scores in place every one of the 32 clubs reconciles exactly on wins, losses, ties, points for and points against. That is the proof the transcription is right and that the missing results were the only thing wrong — a single misread score would break two clubs at once. The committed file still carries the gap, so anything reading data/raw/games.csv is still two rounds behind until the rows are ingested.
  5. Depth of replacement is measurable, but not as asked. The league publishes no depth chart; the club route returns the league's own 404 page, re-checked today. What the league does publish, in consecutive Game Books, is four lists: starters, substitutions, did not play and not active. Measuring how far down those lists a club reached turns an unmeetable requirement into a measurable one. Across Washington and New York from Week 9 to Week 10 this session measured 8 replacements: 6 came from the previous week's substitution list and 2 from the inactive list. It is an ordinal provenance scale, not a coaching depth chart, and it must never be labelled as one.
  6. Two observations are not a correlation. Both starters who came off the previous week's inactive list produced immediately. It is tempting to read that as "a deep replacement is not a negative signal". With n = 2 there is no basis for it, so the number is published with its n and no conclusion is drawn. The only correlation published anywhere on this site is n = 7397: the closing spread against the actual margin, r = 0.4252.
  7. Nothing is live, so the demotion gate has nothing to act on. No strategy in the repository has passed its validation gates. That makes this the right moment to define the gate, before anyone has a result to protect. The honest split is an integrity trigger that fires immediately and a performance trigger that waits for a predeclared sample.
